Ragnar Lothbrok

Ragnar Lothbrok (Old Norse: Ragnarr loðbrók, meaning “Ragnar Hairy-Breeches”) is a legendary Scandinavian warrior, ruler, and hero of the Viking Age. In the traditions, he appears as a powerful king associated with Denmark and Sweden, and as one of the most famous leaders of maritime expeditions in the 9th century.

The stories of Ragnar survive in Old Norse poetry, Icelandic sagas, and medieval chronicles. According to these sources, he led numerous campaigns against the British Isles and the Frankish lands, gaining renown for his boldness and military skill. In the legendary sagas, his father is named as the Swedish king Sigurd Ring.

Although many episodes of his life are mythological in nature, historians believe that the figure of Ragnar may have been shaped from several real Viking chieftains.

Origin and Early History

The Icelandic sagas describe a complex dynastic background for Ragnar. Most sources assert that he was the son of the Swedish ruler Sigurd Ring, a descendant of the legendary Scandinavian king Ivar Vidfamne.

According to these accounts:

  • Ragnar’s grandfather came from the Danish royal line;
  • his family was connected to both Swedish and Danish rulers;
  • after his father’s death, Ragnar inherited power and influence in the region.

Some sources also mention that Ragnar was remarkable in appearance—he is described as a tall, strong, and charismatic man, possessing both physical strength and intelligence.

Early Deeds

The sagas tell that one of Ragnar’s first great deeds was the slaying of a huge serpent or dragon that guarded the domains of Earl Herrauð and his daughter Þóra Borgarhjǫrtr.

To protect himself from the monster’s venom, Ragnar wore a garment made of coarse, shaggy fabric. After his victory over the beast, he acquired the nickname Loðbrók—“Hairy-Breeches.”

As a reward for his feat, he married Þóra.

From this marriage, two sons were born:

  • Erik
  • Agnar

After Þóra’s death, Ragnar’s life took a new turn.

Marriage to Aslaug

Later, Ragnar met a young woman named Kraka, who possessed extraordinary beauty and wisdom. She lived in Norway with a poor peasant family.

Ragnar decided to test her intelligence and ordered her to appear before him:

  • neither clothed nor unclothed,
  • neither fasting nor fed,
  • neither alone nor in company.

Kraka fulfilled the challenge in an unusual manner:

  • she came wrapped in a fishing net,
  • biting into an onion,
  • accompanied only by a dog.

Impressed by her cleverness, Ragnar took her as his wife. It was later revealed that Kraka was in fact Aslaug—the daughter of the legendary hero Sigurd Fafnir’s Bane.

Ragnar’s Sons

Aslaug bore Ragnar several famous sons who became heroes of many sagas:

  • Ivar the Boneless
  • Björn Ironside
  • Sigurd Snake-in-the-Eye
  • Hvitserk
  • Ragnvald

These warriors later gained renown as leaders of major Viking expeditions.

The Great Heathen Army

According to the chronicles, in 865 the sons of Ragnar assembled a massive Viking force known as the Great Heathen Army.

The campaign’s goal was to avenge the death of their father, whom, according to legend, King Ælla of Northumbria had executed by throwing him into a pit of snakes.

The Viking army invaded England and seized vast territories. Some historians believe that these events led to the establishment of the Danelaw—the regions where Scandinavian laws and customs prevailed.

Ragnar in Danish Chronicles

Danish sources present a somewhat different picture.

The chronicler Saxo Grammaticus, in his work Gesta Danorum, describes Ragnar as a powerful Danish king and an outstanding military commander. In his version, Ragnar:

  • undertakes numerous expeditions across Europe,
  • conquers parts of Norway and Sweden,
  • wages wars in Britain and the Baltic region.

In these accounts, Ragnar sometimes appears not only as a hero but also as a stern ruler for whom war and conquest are the primary means of consolidating power.

The Raid on Paris

Some scholars connect Ragnar with the historical Viking leader Reginherus, who led an attack on Paris in 845.

During that campaign:

  • the Viking fleet sailed up the Seine,
  • the Frankish forces were defeated,
  • captured prisoners were sacrificed to Odin.

In the end, the Frankish king Charles the Bald was compelled to pay a large ransom to make the Vikings withdraw.

Ragnar’s Death

The most famous version of his death involves the English king Ælla of Northumbria.

According to legend:

  1. Ragnar sailed to England with a small fleet.
  2. His army was defeated.
  3. King Ælla ordered the captive thrown into a pit filled with venomous snakes.

Before his death, Ragnar is said to have uttered famous words that his sons would surely avenge him.

Ragnar in Skaldic Poetry

The figure of Ragnar also appears in Old Norse poetry. For example:

  • the poem Krákumál describes Ragnar’s final words before death;
  • he speaks of soon arriving in Valhalla, where the Valkyries will greet him;
  • the hero dies laughing, proud of his life as a warrior.

Such motifs underscore the ideals of Scandinavian culture—courage, honor, and a readiness to die in battle.

Historical Significance

Although Ragnar’s historicity remains a subject of debate, his figure has become emblematic of the Viking Age.

He embodies:

  • the pursuit of glory and wealth,
  • maritime expeditions and conquests,
  • the authority of the warrior aristocracy.

Even if Ragnar himself is a legendary figure, his supposed sons—such as Ivar the Boneless and Björn Ironside—are mentioned in actual historical sources.

Legacy and Influence

The stories of Ragnar have had a profound impact on Scandinavian culture and literature. His image became the archetype of the ideal Viking:

  • a fearless warrior
  • a brilliant commander
  • a charismatic leader

In later eras, the legends of Ragnar were used as symbols of national pride in the Scandinavian countries. Today, his name is widely known through books, historical research, and popular television series about the Vikings.
