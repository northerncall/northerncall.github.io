When we speak of shamanism, the first image that comes to many people’s minds is that of shamans using mushrooms or other “sacred plants” to enter a trance.

It is true that psychoactive mushrooms held an important place in the traditions of various peoples—from Siberian and northern tribes to Native Americans. But the reality is that **shamanism is not reducible to mushrooms**, and not every shaman used them.

> Mushrooms are just one tool. A powerful, striking tool, but not the only one. The shamanic path is far broader.

So why did shamans turn to mushrooms? Simply put, they offered a quick and powerful way to “switch” perception. The mushroom acted as a key to the doors of other worlds. But every key has a flip side—if you are not ready, the door may slam shut or lead somewhere unintended.

In tradition, mushrooms were perceived not as food or as a “high,” but as living spirit allies. Each species had its own character. Approaching them without preparation was considered dangerous. And here lies an important point: shamans knew that the mushroom was not the power itself, but only a conduit to power. If a shaman was unprepared, if he lacked an inner core, the spirit of the mushroom could deceive, frighten, or even harm him. For this reason, in many traditions, mushrooms were reserved for experienced shamans rather than being a daily practice.

> Moreover, many shamans did without mushrooms altogether. They believed that true power came from within: from the ability to hear the world, to maintain a connection with one’s lineage, to master one’s body and spirit. For them, mushrooms were a crutch, an accelerator, but not a necessary path.

## Psilocybin Mushrooms

Many have heard of psilocybin mushrooms—some call them “magic,” others “hallucinogenic.” But in reality, they are not simply a “recreational drug”; they represent an entire stratum of culture, science, and spiritual practice.

Psilocybin mushrooms have been known to humanity for millennia. In Mexico, Mayan and Aztec shamans used them to communicate with gods and the spirit world. There are depictions of “mushroom stones” over 2,000 years old. Even in Europe and Siberia, traces of mushroom use in shamanic rituals have been found.

### A Little Science about Psilocybin Mushrooms

The main active compound in these mushrooms is **psilocybin**, which is converted in the body into **psilocin**. It is psilocin that alters perception.

**How it works:** psilocin acts on serotonin receptors in the brain, particularly the so‑called *5‑HT2A* receptors. As a result, the usual “networks” of the brain temporarily operate less rigidly than normal. The person begins to see the world differently—both literally and figuratively.

What a person experiences under psilocybin:
- Intensified colors and sounds
- A sense of merging with the surrounding world
- Altered perception of time
- Surges of emotion—from euphoria to tears

Physiologically, psilocybin is considered relatively safe: it does not cause physical dependence and does not damage organs. However, there are psychological risks—for example, it can provoke psychotic episodes, intensify derealization, and trigger subsequent depression.

### Psilocybin and Shamanic Experience

For shamanic tradition, this mushroom is not merely a means to alter consciousness; it is a sacred guide. For millennia, shamans have used it to communicate with gods, ancestors, and nature spirits. To this day, many practitioners view it not as a “substance” but as the spirit of the mushroom with which one can enter into contact.

In Mesoamerica, psilocybin mushrooms were called **“teonanácatl”** —**“flesh of the gods.”** They were eaten during rituals to make contact with higher powers, receive prophecy, or achieve healing. Mayan and Aztec shamans believed that the mushroom opened “second sight” and allowed one to see what was hidden in ordinary states.

The shamanic experience with psilocybin can be described as a journey of the soul. The person feels that their body remains here, while their consciousness moves beyond the familiar world.
Several levels of experience are often distinguished:
- purification—often accompanied by bodily sensations, mild nausea, and internal resistance.
- dissolution of boundaries—the world begins to merge into unity; the sense of “self and other” disappears.
- encounter with spirits—shamans describe the appearance of entities, teachers, or ancestors who impart knowledge.
- return—when the experience ends, the person feels that they have received a lesson or a message and must “ground” it in life.

The psilocybin experience is often called “educational.” In shamanic tradition, the mushroom is seen as a mentor that can show a person:
- their true fears and how to overcome them.
- hidden traumas that need healing.
- ways to connect with lineage and nature.
- a sense of unity with the whole world.

But it is important to understand: the mushroom is not always “benevolent.” It can deliver harsh lessons, forcing shadows and traumas to the surface. Shamans call this “the trial of the mushroom spirit.” If a person is not ready, they may fall into fear, panic, or become lost in the images.

For this reason, in traditional cultures a shaman‑mentor always guided the process. Taking it alone was considered dangerous—like traveling into the wild forest without a guide.

## Amanita muscaria (Fly Agaric)

The red fly agaric is mentioned in the myths and rituals of many northern peoples. Siberian shamans used it to enter trance states, communicate with spirits, and journey to other worlds. In some regions, fly agaric was even used in everyday life—as a painkiller, a remedy for fatigue, and a “body cleanser.”

European culture also preserved traces: fairy‑tale characters are often depicted sitting on fly agaric caps, and the tradition of Christmas decorations may be connected to this mushroom (red and white colors, reindeer, shamanic journeys).

### A Little Science about Amanita muscaria

The main active compounds in fly agaric are muscarine, ibotenic acid, and muscimol.

- Ibotenic acid partially converts to muscimol when dried—it is muscimol that induces dream‑like states and altered perception.
- Muscarine is present in small amounts and does not play a major role.

The effect of fly agaric strongly depends on dosage, preparation method (raw, dried, tincture), and individual physiology. The experience can vary widely. Some feel relaxation and vivid dreams; others experience strong intoxication or even hallucinations. Interestingly, fly agaric does not produce “classic” hallucinations like psilocybin mushrooms. Its effect is closer to a trance‑like, dream‑like state.

Possible effects:
- a feeling of lightness or, conversely, heaviness in the body.
- distortion of spatial and temporal perception.
- vivid dreams and “out‑of‑body” sensations.
- nausea, vomiting, sweating in case of overdose.

Muscimol is of particular interest: it acts as a GABA receptor agonist and can influence sleep, anxiety, and brain function. Some researchers suggest that low doses may help with insomnia and nervous disorders.

Unlike psilocybin, Amanita muscaria is not prohibited in most countries. It can be foraged and even ordered dried online. However, in some countries (e.g., Latvia and Romania) it is listed as a controlled substance.

### Amanita muscaria and Shamanic Experience

Unlike psilocybin, it does not produce vivid hallucinations; rather, it transports the person into a dream‑like space. For the shaman, this is not entertainment but a tool: through fly agaric, he enters contact with spirits, journeys between worlds, and receives knowledge.

The shamanic experience with fly agaric differs from that with psilocybin. Whereas psilocybin opens perception and amplifies emotions, fly agaric seems to “detach” consciousness from the body.
The main stages of the experience:
- bodily changes—heaviness, warmth, unusual muscular sensations.
- waking dreams—images appear as if in a dream, yet the person remains aware.
- soul journey—the shaman feels that he has “left” the body and entered another world.
- contact with spirits—entities appear: animal guides, ancestral spirits, or deities.
- return—the body feels heavy, but the experience remains as a dream that cannot be forgotten.

Amanita muscaria is not a “fairy‑tale mushroom” but an ancient shamanic tool. It opens the gates to the spirit worlds, but it does so not gently but severely. Its experience can be compared to immersion in a dream, where each vision is a lesson.

For the shaman, fly agaric is not a dangerous toy but an ally to be approached with respect and caution.

## Amanita pantherina (Panther Cap)

If the red fly agaric is widely known as the “fairy‑tale mushroom,” its relative—the panther cap—is far less familiar, yet far more insidious. It resembles the red fly agaric, but its cap is brown with white spots. The panther cap is no less beautiful, but it is known for a much stronger and more dangerous effect.

Historical references to the panther cap are fewer than for the red fly agaric. It was rarely used in rituals or shamanic practices precisely because of its unpredictability and high toxicity. Nevertheless, in some regions of Europe it was known and feared. In the scientific literature of the 19th and 20th centuries, the panther cap was described as a potent poison.

### A Little Science about Amanita pantherina

Like the red fly agaric, the panther cap contains ibotenic acid and muscimol. However, the concentration of these substances is significantly higher, which means the effect is stronger and more abrupt.

Characteristics of the panther cap:
- higher risk of poisoning.
- faster onset of action.
- effects are “harsher” and often unpredictable.

Experiences with the panther cap are described variably:
- strong perceptual changes (distortion of space, vivid visual imagery).
- feelings of leaving the body or a “splitting” of consciousness.
- sudden transitions from euphoria to anxiety.
- loss of coordination, intense drowsiness or, conversely, agitation.
- nausea, vomiting, convulsions.
- pupil constriction, sweating.

Unlike the red fly agaric, the panther cap was hardly ever used in traditional medicine. Only rare references to external application exist; it was almost never taken internally due to the high risk.

Scientific data on the panther cap is scarce. Most scholarly attention focuses on its toxicology. Interest arises among neuropharmacologists: muscimol may hold promise for research into sleep, trance states, and nervous system regulation. But because of its high danger, the mushroom itself remains largely unstudied in clinical medicine.

Like the red fly agaric, the panther cap is not prohibited in most countries, although it is considered a poisonous mushroom. However, in some countries (e.g., Latvia and Romania) it has been placed on lists of controlled substances.

### Amanita pantherina and Shamanic Experience

In shamanic practices, this mushroom was considered a far more severe and unpredictable ally. Its action is stronger, deeper, and more dangerous. It was occasionally used only by experienced practitioners capable of enduring its harsh trials.

In ethnographic accounts from the 19th century, it was called the “poisonous shamanic mushroom” and associated with rituals that demanded particular harshness: exorcism of evil spirits, work with illness, or testing the strength of an apprentice.

The experience with the panther cap is described as more “dark” and abrupt than with the red fly agaric.
The main stages of the experience:
- intense bodily impact—heaviness, weakness, loss of coordination.
- deep trance—consciousness “disconnects” from ordinary perception and moves to another layer.
- dream‑like space—images arise, but they are often frightening, somber, archetypal.
- encounter with spirits of trial—contact with harsh entities that test strength and readiness.
- return—often accompanied by exhaustion and a sense of having “survived a trial.”

In shamanic tradition, the panther cap was regarded as a tester. If the red fly agaric is an ally, teacher, and guide, the panther cap is the examiner who tests inner strength.

## Summary

Each mushroom is a distinct spirit, a distinct path.

- Psilocybin opens the heart and mind, revealing harmony.
- Amanita muscaria offers a key to the shamanic world and helps in contact with powers.
- Amanita pantherina tests one’s fortitude and leads through darkness.

All three paths lead to expanded consciousness, but each in its own way: through light, through knowledge, or through trial. And the overarching lesson is this—the power of mushrooms lies not in the substances themselves, but in how we learn to listen to them and respect them. True shamanic power has always been and remains within the person: in the ability to communicate with spirits, master states of consciousness, and hear the call of the world.
