## Aslaug

**Aslaug** (Old Norse: **Áslaug** [ˈɑːsˌlɔuɣ]), also known as **Aslög**, **Aslǫg**, **Kraka** (Old Norse: [ˈkrɑːkɑ]) or **Kráka**, is a figure in Norse mythology. She is mentioned in the **Prose Edda**, the **Völsunga Saga**, and the **Saga of Ragnar Lothbrok** as one of Ragnar’s wives.

---

## Aslaug in Legend

According to the **13th-century *Saga of Ragnar Lothbrok***, Aslaug was the daughter of **Sigurd** and the shieldmaiden **Brynhildr**, but she was raised by Brynhildr’s foster father, **Heimir**.

After the deaths of Sigurd and Brynhildr, Heimir became fearful for the girl’s safety. Therefore, he made a **large harp**, inside which the child could be concealed. He then set out on a journey, pretending to be a poor harper, and carried with him the harp in which Aslaug was hidden.

They arrived at **Spangereid** in the district of **Lindesnes** in Norway, where they stayed for the night in the house of the peasants **Åke** and **Grima**. Åke suspected that valuable items were hidden inside the harp and told his wife. Grima persuaded him to **kill Heimir** while he slept.

However, when they broke open the harp, they found a **little girl** inside. They decided to raise her as their own daughter and gave her the name **Kraka** (“Crow”).

To conceal her beauty—which was considered a sign of noble birth—they **smothered her in tar** and dressed her in a long hood.

But one day, while Kraka was bathing, she was seen by **warriors of the legendary king Ragnar Lothbrok**. They were so struck by her beauty that they forgot about the bread they were baking, and it burned. When Ragnar asked why this had happened, they told him about the girl.

Ragnar sent for her but decided to **test her wits**. He ordered her to appear:

- **neither clothed nor unclothed**,  
- **neither fasting nor eating**,  
- **neither alone nor in company**.

Kraka arrived:

- **wrapped in a fishing net**,  
- **eating an onion**,  
- **accompanied only by a dog**.

Impressed by her resourcefulness and wisdom, Ragnar **asked her to become his wife**.

## Scholarly Research

According to the scholar **Marilyn Jurich**, the story of Aslaug in the *Saga of Ragnar Lothbrok* is a prototype of the folktale motif **“The Clever Peasant Girl”** (Aarne–Thompson classification **No. 875**).

The plot of the saga aligns almost entirely with this folklore type up to the point of Aslaug’s marriage to Ragnar. Yet the similarities continue further. The saga emphasizes:

- **Aslaug’s resolve** (she refuses intimacy with Ragnar before the wedding);  
- her **almost supernatural wisdom**.

When Ragnar insisted on intimacy **immediately after the wedding**, against her advice, their first son **Ivar** was born weak and “**without bones**.”

## Comparison with Other Tales

The **Brothers Grimm** discussed the similarity between their tale **No. 8 (1815) “The Clever Farmer’s Daughter” (*Die kluge Bauerntochter*)** and the northern story of Kraka. They mention this similarity in the appendix to the text.

They also note a similarity to a story from **Johannes Pauli**’s collection ***Schimpf und Ernst*** (1519–1522).

---

## In Other Works

The romantic poem **“The Fostering of Aslaug”** by **William Morris** retells the story of her relationship with Ragnar. The poem is based on the version of the tale from **Benjamin Thorpe’s *Northern Mythology* (1851)**.

Morris changes the narrative tone, giving it a **romantic character**: he removes the darker and more complex elements of the saga and portrays Ragnar as a **classical hero wooing a maiden**.

Aslaug also appears in **Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué**’s work ***Aslauga’s Knight***, published in 1810 together with two other Icelandic romances in the collection ***The Hero of the North* (*Der Held des Nordens*)**.
