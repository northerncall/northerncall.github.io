**Blenda** is a legendary heroine in Swedish folklore, best known from the tradition of **Blendasägnen**, which originates in the historical province of **Småland** in southern Sweden. According to the tale, she led the women of the **Värend** region and orchestrated a cunning attack on a Danish army that had invaded the defenseless lands.

The legend has become an important part of the region’s heritage and a symbol of female courage and resourcefulness in Scandinavian epic tradition.

---

# The Legend of Blenda

The tradition places the events during the reign of King **Alle** (in some versions identified with the Anglo-Saxon ruler Ælla). At that time, the king of the Geats led a large army to war against Norway.

A great number of men joined the campaign:

- the Western Geats,
- the Southern Geats,
- the people of Småland.

As a result, many settlements were left almost entirely without defenders.

The **Danes** seized upon this vulnerability and decided to raid the depopulated lands.

---

# Blenda’s Call

At that time, a noblewoman named Blenda lived in the district of **Konga**. Realising there were hardly any men left to defend their homeland, she resolved to act on her own.

According to the legend, she sent out a **fiery cross**—an ancient symbol of alarm and mobilisation—to summon all women to defend their land.

Women from several districts answered her call:

- Konga
- Albo
- Kinnevald
- Norrvidinge
- Uppvidinge

They gathered on the plain of **Brovellir**, which local tradition places in the Värend region.

---

# A Ruse Against the Invaders

As the Danish army approached, the women decided to use deception rather than force.

They approached the Danes and expressed admiration for their bravery and strength. Then they invited the warriors to a feast in honour of their victory.

At the celebration, the Danes were generously served:

- meat,
- bread,
- mead and beer.

After the long feast, the warriors grew drunk and fell asleep.

The women then attacked and slaughtered all the invaders, using:

- axes,
- clubs,
- farming tools.

According to tradition, not a single Danish warrior survived.

---

# Privileges Granted to the Women of Värend

When King Alle returned from his campaign and learned what had happened, he was deeply impressed by the women’s courage.

In gratitude, he granted them special privileges, including:

- **equal inheritance rights** alongside their brothers and husbands;
- the right to wear a **belt around the waist** as a symbol of being ever ready to defend their homes;
- participation in rituals and festivities, including the right to **beat the wedding drum**.

The five districts that took part in the defense were united into the territory of **Värend**, a name often associated with the word for “defense” or “protection.”

In honour of the heroine:

- her village was named **Värnslanda**;
- a settlement called **Bländinge** arose near the site of the battle.

---

# Historical Basis of the Legend

The first written records of the legend appeared relatively late. It was documented in print only in the 17th century.

The jurist and historian **Johan Stiernhöök**, in his work *De iure sueonum et gothorum vetusto* (1672), connected the story of Blenda with ancient inheritance rights. He claimed that such rights had been granted to women after the **Battle of Brávellir**, where they had shown courage.

Later, the legend was actively used in legal and public debates during the 17th century, when the people of the region defended their distinctive inheritance customs.

---

# The Role of Petter Rudebeck

Scholars believe that the legend took its final form thanks to the historian **Petter Rudebeck** (1660–1710).

He collected the folk traditions of Småland, including:

- customs,
- local legends,
- oral traditions.

Many of the texts recounting the story of Blenda are written in a language and style characteristic of his work.

---

# Blenda in Literature

In the 19th century, the legend gained new popularity through Romantic literature.

In 1813, the poet **Erik Johan Stagnelius** wrote the poem **“Blenda”**, transforming the folk tradition into a heroic epic.

---

# Possible Historical Core

If the events indeed had a historical basis, they may have occurred around the year **500**. During this period, Scandinavian societies still preserved traditions that allowed women to take part in warfare.

Historical sources and sagas mention so-called **shieldmaidens**—female warriors who fought in battles.

For example, some accounts state that about **three hundred women** fought in the famous **Battle of Brávellir** around the 8th century.

---

# Attempts to Explain the Legend

Historians have repeatedly tried to link the legend to real events. Hypotheses include:

- a war preceding the meeting of Scandinavian kings at **Kungahälla** in 1101;
- the attack by the Norwegian king **Sigurd I** on Kalmar in 1123;
- the Danish invasion of Sweden by King **Sven III** in the 12th century;
- conflicts of the 13th century during the reign of the Danish king **Eric V**.

However, there is also a more pragmatic interpretation: the legend may have been created later to explain the unusual tradition of the Värend region, where women enjoyed **equal inheritance rights**.

---

# Significance of the Legend

Today, the story of Blenda is regarded as an important part of Småland’s cultural heritage.

It symbolises:

- the strength and determination of women,
- the ability to organise collective self-defense,
- the role of folk memory in shaping regional identity.

Although the historical veracity of the event remains debatable, the legend of Blenda continues to live on in Swedish culture as an example of courage, cleverness, and communal unity.
