In the traditional religion of the Scandinavians, the rite of **blót** was not merely a sacrifice but a form of **mutual agreement between human and deity**. The foundation of this interaction was considered to be a personal relationship, described in Old Norse texts by the word **vinátta** — friendship or alliance.

Such relationships were built not on abstract faith or dogma, but on the principle of **mutual benefit and obligation**. The human offered gifts to the gods, and in return the deity bestowed protection, luck, defense, or success in one’s endeavors.

---

# Personal Bonds with the Gods

In the sagas, the gods are often depicted not as distant, unapproachable forces, but as **personal patrons** with whom one could establish a special connection. The term **fulltrúi** was used to describe such patronage — a kind of “trusted guardian” or divine ally.

These relationships could:

- arise through offerings and prayers;
- be strengthened by rituals and oaths;
- break apart if one of the parties failed to meet expectations.

The sagas frequently contain stories of people **abandoning their former patron god** and turning to another if they believed the former no longer brought them luck.

---

# Social Model of Relations

This type of interaction mirrors the structure of relationships in Scandinavian society. For example, it resembles the bond between a **goði** (a religious and political leader) and his supporters at the assembly — **þingmenn**.

This system was built on:

- mutual support,
- obligations,
- an exchange of services and gifts.

If one participant in the alliance ceased to fulfill their role, the bond could be broken.

---

# The Principle of “Gift for Gift”

At the heart of the pagan cult lay the ancient principle of **reciprocal gift-giving**.

It is reflected in the well-known stanza from the **Hávamál**, which states that **every gift should be worthy of the one who receives it**.

The meaning of this idea can be expressed as:

- a small gift yields a small result;
- a generous offering may bring a great reward.

In other words, interaction with the divine was viewed as a **balance between what a person gives and what they hope to receive**.

---

# A Practical Approach to Ritual

In traditional Scandinavian culture, blót was understood in a highly pragmatic way.

People made sacrifices for specific goals:

- victory in battle;
- a good harvest;
- a safe sea voyage;
- success in trade or politics.

Thus, the choice of offering, the timing of the rite, and which deity to address were carefully considered.

---

# Conclusion

Old Norse religion was to a great extent founded on **contractual relationships between humans and the gods**.

Blót served as the instrument for maintaining these relationships and reflected a broader cultural principle in Scandinavian society — **the exchange of gifts, mutual loyalty, and practical advantage**.

This is why, in the sources, we see people:

- forming an alliance with a deity,
- strengthening it through offerings,
- or severing the bond when it no longer proved beneficial.
